---

**Syed:** I'm a dual major in Computer Engineering and Physics. It's an unusual combination, but honestly it's one of the things I'm most proud of — I feel like it gives me a unique lens when approaching problems. On the engineering side I'm comfortable with data analysis, computational modeling, and systems thinking. On the physics side I've built a strong foundation in how matter and energy behave at a fundamental level. I find that the two disciplines reinforce each other in ways people don't always expect.

**Dr. Lovitt:** That's an interesting combination. How did you end up choosing both?

**Syed:** Honestly, I couldn't let go of either. But what's really drawn me deeper into both fields is how inseparable physics and chemistry actually are. At their core, chemistry *is* physics — chemical bonding, molecular structure, reaction dynamics, thermodynamics — all of it is grounded in quantum mechanics and electromagnetism. When you study how electrons behave in an atom, you're doing physics. When you ask what that means for how two atoms bond or how energy is released in a reaction, you've crossed into chemistry. The line between them is mostly a curriculum boundary, not a natural one. Physical chemistry exists precisely because those two fields can't really be separated. My physics background actually makes me better at understanding the *why* behind chemical phenomena, not just the *what*. And that's part of why I'm drawn to research — it's where those boundaries dissolve completely.

---

**Syed:** After going through your website and publications, the work that stood out to me most was your research on integrating climate change and social context into chemistry learning, and your work on belonging in gateway STEM courses. I'm really interested in how scientific education can be made more accessible and engaging — especially for students from diverse backgrounds. The POGIL-based collaborative learning research also caught my attention because it connects to something I care about: how the *structure* of a learning environment shapes outcomes.

**Dr. Lovitt:** I'm glad those resonated with you. What draws you to the education side specifically — are you interested in chemistry education as a research field, or more as a means to study something broader?

**Syed:** Honestly, a bit of both. I'm genuinely curious about the pedagogy itself, but I'm also interested in the interdisciplinary nature of your work — the way it pulls in systems thinking, social sciences, and equity frameworks. That kind of problem feels like it needs exactly the kind of cross-disciplinary thinking I've been building.

---

**Syed:** I also want to be transparent about where I'm headed. I'm seriously considering graduate school after my undergrad — I'm drawn to fields at the intersection of physics and chemistry, potentially physical chemistry or chemical physics. I see this research opportunity not just as something to put on an application, but as a way to figure out what kind of researcher I want to be and whether academia is the right path for me.

**Dr. Lovitt:** That's a mature way to think about it. Graduate school is a big commitment — it's good that you're approaching it deliberately. What draws you to physical chemistry specifically?

**Syed:** The fundamental nature of the questions — understanding matter and energy at a deep level. I'm also interested in how computational methods are reshaping the field, which connects back to my engineering background. I'd love to eventually do research that bridges simulation, experiment, and real-world application.

---

**Syed:** I did want to ask you something that's been on my mind. If I were to get involved in one of your projects — how does the path to publication typically work for undergraduate researchers? Is that something that's realistic, and what would it take to get there?

**Dr. Lovitt:** It's definitely possible, and it's a great question to ask early. For undergraduates, it usually means sustained involvement over at least a semester or two — contributing meaningfully to data collection, analysis, or writing. In education research specifically, undergrads often contribute to study design, interviews, coding qualitative data, or literature reviews. If the contribution is substantial enough, co-authorship is absolutely on the table. The key is consistency and taking ownership of a piece of the work.

**Syed:** That's really helpful to know. I'm willing to put in that kind of time. I'd rather be deeply involved in one project than spread thin across several.

---

**Dr. Lovitt:** I appreciate that. Here's what I'd suggest — take a look at a couple of my recent papers, particularly the climate change and belonging pieces, and come back to me with a short note about where you think you could contribute and what skills you'd bring to that work. That'll help me figure out if there's a good fit with what's currently active.

**Syed:** Absolutely, I can do that. Is there a timeline you're working with for bringing someone on this summer?

**Dr. Lovitt:** I'd like to have something figured out in the next few weeks. So if you can get me that note by end of next week, we can go from there.

**Syed:** Perfect. Thank you again, Dr. Lovitt — I'm really excited about the possibility of working with you. I'll follow up by next Friday.
